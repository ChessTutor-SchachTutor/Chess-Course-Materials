# My Handbook of Strategy Games

*The handbook is currentyl in translation, and is expected to be here mid November 26. Please bear with me a little longer.*

I developed and wrote this handbook in Germany, originally in German, and have also translated it into English to make it accessible to more people.

I have worked to give this handbook as sound a scientific foundation as I could. My central point as a former chess coach and a schoolteacher is this: In my view, these strategy games are precisely the way to develop the skills that parents and educators hope to encourage when they send a child to chess lessons.

They allow children to practise individual aspects of chess thinking without first having to spend one or two years learning the foundations of chess. That is exactly why I consider this approach so valuable. In my experience, many children stop playing after attending a school chess club without ever reaching the point where they can consciously think through and reflect on their decisions on the board. These simplified strategy games are designed for exactly this purpose: Children can explore decisions and their consequences at a level they can understand.

## What the handbook contains

There is a theoretical section of around 30 pages in which I try to explain my thinking. Among other things, I compare chess as a school subject in Armenia with school chess clubs in Germany and explain why the two are fundamentally different, and why neither should be confused with simply introducing children to chess.

This is followed by my attempt to put these ideas into practice: a selection and detailed analysis of 30 strategy games that I consider useful for this purpose.

The final section documents my work at the Hector Children's Academy in Ulm, Germany, an enrichment programme for particularly gifted primary school children who do not necessarily have any previous chess experience. I have run the programme there several times, and some of the results surprised even me.

## Learning to think through games – and why this even helps chess players

The programme develops chess thinking. It is therefore particularly suitable for children who are sent to chess primarily for the hoped-for benefits to their thinking, rather than to learn the game of chess itself. In my experience, this applies to the vast majority of children attending school chess clubs. These strategy games pursue that goal just as playfully: Children play, face challenges, and learn to think consciously about their decisions.

**The main focus is therefore on children for whom developing their thinking is the priority. But even those who want to become chess players benefit.** These games explicitly teach systematic thinking – and that is just as important later in chess.

It is a different approach: Rather than first getting to know as much of chess as possible, children use manageable games to learn how to approach a position systematically, examine possibilities, and explain their decisions. This is closer to the understanding of chess education that I associate with the Soviet chess school and that also underpins the rest of my chess course.

I have also used this approach successfully in chess coaching. For example, I helped a beginner reach 1700 Elo within two years, with one-hour lessons with me and less than an hour of independent training per day. I was specifically paid my usual coaching rates to teach this programme, and the family is extremely happy with the child's progress.

For me, this is therefore no detour on the way to chess. It lays a foundation that is just as necessary on the chessboard later.

Those who want to learn chess afterwards can build on it. Those who stop have still practised thinking at an appropriate level – and that is exactly where I see the value of this programme.

## Why is it free?

Developing, writing, and checking this handbook took me six full months of work. I want to make that work available to you to support the meaningful development of your children.

**It is deliberately free for everyone. But please do not mistake free for worthless.** A great deal of work has gone into it, along with my conviction that this approach can give children something valuable.
